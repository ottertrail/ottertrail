# Backend telemetry: traces, logs, and metrics

**Status: stage 3 of the [roadmap](roadmap.md). Receiving and storing, exceptions in the error list, and the dashboard's Traces, Logs, Metrics, Services, Databases, and Hosts pages (steps 1 to 3 of [How it is built](#how-it-is-built)) are built.**

A site's servers send what they did, in the OpenTelemetry protocol (OTLP): traces (each request through the services it touched, as spans), logs, and metrics. Ottertrail keeps them in ClickHouse, beside the browser's events, and the dashboard shows each with the others: a slow request's trace and its logs, an exception on a span in the same error list as the browser's errors. Any OpenTelemetry SDK or Collector can send to it; nothing of Ottertrail's own runs on the servers.

## Receiving

OTLP/HTTP, version 1 ([specification](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otlp/), 1.11.1 when this was written), at the paths an exporter appends to its base address, so pointing one at the instance is enough:

```text
POST /v1/traces
POST /v1/logs
POST /v1/metrics
```

```sh
OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT=https://analytics.example.com
OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_HEADERS=Authorization=Bearer%20sk_…
OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_PROTOCOL=http/protobuf
```

- **Both encodings:** protobuf (`application/x-protobuf`, exporters' default) and JSON (`application/json`, with the specification's rules: trace and span IDs in hex, enums as numbers, 64-bit numbers as strings), gzip-compressed or not. The answer comes in the request's encoding.
- **The project's secret key,** `Authorization: Bearer sk_…`, as server-side events take it ([web analytics](web-analytics.md#server-side-events)). Never a public key: these come from servers.
- **Decoding:** [protobufjs](https://github.com/protobufjs/protobuf.js) (BSD-3-Clause) with the official message definitions from [opentelemetry-proto](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-proto) (Apache-2.0), kept in the repository at a pinned version. OpenTelemetry's own JavaScript package encodes requests but does not decode them, and is marked for internal use.
- **gRPC is not in the first version:** every OpenTelemetry SDK and the Collector can send over HTTP, and a Collector can take gRPC from anything that only speaks that.

## Answers

As the specification has them, so exporters retry what may succeed later and drop what never will:

| Case                                                                      | Answer                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Stored                                                                    | 200, an empty `Export…ServiceResponse`                                                   |
| Stored, but some records refused (below)                                  | 200, with `partial_success`: how many were refused and why. Exporters do not retry these |
| Malformed: not OTLP, or not the encoding it says                          | 400                                                                                      |
| No key, or one no project has                                             | 401                                                                                      |
| Over 16 MiB once unpacked, or over a million messages                     | 413: send smaller batches                                                                |
| Costs more than a project's whole budget                                  | 413: send smaller batches                                                                |
| Another content type, or a compression not gzip                           | 415                                                                                      |
| Over the project's or the instance's budget                               | 429, with `Retry-After`                                                                  |
| The project has 4 requests in progress on this process                    | 429, with `Retry-After`                                                                  |
| ClickHouse cannot take it now, or this process has 8 requests in progress | 503, with `Retry-After`                                                                  |

Exporters retry 429, 502, 503, and 504, and nothing else.

A request counts as in progress from when its key is checked until its work is done. Until its handler starts (the body still uploading, or refused before), that is when its answer is sent or its connection closes. After, it is when the handler finishes, its insert included, whether or not the answer could be sent: a client that disconnects while its records are being written keeps its place until the insert settles, so an exporter that times out and retries cannot pile a second body on the first. The limits bound memory, since each request holds its decoded body until then (`apps/api/src/collection/in-flight.ts`, tested in `telemetry.test.ts` with a client that hangs up while ClickHouse holds the insert).

## Stored straight in ClickHouse

Decided on 18 November 2026. Analytics events then landed in PostgreSQL first, which confirmed them, and were copied to ClickHouse. Telemetry did not: it is the largest data Ottertrail takes, often many times a site's events, and writing it twice would double the cost of the busiest path. Events have gone straight to ClickHouse the same way since ([ClickHouse](clickhouse.md)). An OTLP request is confirmed once ClickHouse has written it to disk (asynchronous inserts, waiting for the write, with `fsync_after_insert` and `fsync_part_directory` on every telemetry table since migration `0010_telemetry_fsync`, as on events), so telemetry the exporter heard was stored survives a power loss; while ClickHouse cannot take it the answer is 503, which every exporter retries, keeping the data in its queue meanwhile.

A retry after an answer that was lost stores a record twice. Each record has an ID, a span its trace and span IDs, a log record or data point a hash of its content, and the tables keep one row per ID (`ReplacingMergeTree`); what counts records reads them deduplicated, except the large aggregates of spans ([how the pages read](#how-the-pages-read)). One exception: a log record with neither a time nor an observed time takes the time it arrived, which differs on a retry, so a retry of it stays twice. OpenTelemetry's SDKs and the Collector always set the observed time.

## Storage

Four tables, partitioned by day. Spans and logs are ordered by project, then time, then the record's ID, so a project's range is one read and every query filters by project first. Metrics are ordered by project, then name, then time: a chart reads one metric, and an export holds all of a process's metrics at one time, so ordered by time alone a chart read every metric's rows of the range.

- **Every table:** when the record happened and when its request arrived (`received_at`, as events have it). Spans and metric rows carry the time the API received the request, by its clock (migration `0011_received_at`; rows stored before it read 1970); logs carry theirs from migration 0005 (below).
- **`spans`:** trace, span, and parent IDs, name, kind, start, duration, status and its message, the service's name and version, environment, attributes, events, links, and the instrumentation scope (never AI prompts or completions: [below](#ai-prompts-and-completions-are-not-stored)); and a database call's system, database, and query shape, worked out by ClickHouse when the span is written ([databases](#worked-out-when-a-span-is-written)).
- **`logs`:** time and observed time, when it arrived (`received_at`, ClickHouse's clock as it writes the record, with a minmax index, for the live view: migration `0005_log_received_at`), severity number and text, body, trace and span IDs, service, environment, attributes, scope, event name (again without AI prompts or completions).
- **`metric_points`:** gauges and sums: name, unit, kind, temporality, monotonic or not, start and time, value, attributes, service, environment, and the series (below).
- **`metric_histograms`:** histograms with explicit buckets: bounds, counts, count, sum, minimum, maximum, and the series.

ClickHouse merges a table's parts when it decides, and never promises to: a day that gets no more inserts can keep a retried copy in a part of its own for its whole retention. So every telemetry table merges a day's parts into one once all of them are an hour old (`min_age_to_force_merge_seconds = 3600` with `min_age_to_force_merge_on_partition_only = 1`, migration `0002_force_telemetry_merges`): a copy is gone within about an hour of its day's last insert, plus the merge's own time. A day still being written to merges as ClickHouse decides until then. `telemetry-retention.test.ts` shows a day of 1,000,000 spans and a 10-span copy merged on its own once its parts pass the age (cut to a second in the test), while the same day without the setting keeps both parts and counts 1,000,010 rows.

A metric row's `series` is a `cityHash64` of its service, scope name and version, attributes, resource attributes, and temporality (a histogram's also its bounds), a `MATERIALIZED` column worked out when the row is written, so a chart does not hash every row's attribute maps.

The service comes from the resource's `service.name`, the release from `service.version`, the environment from `deployment.environment.name` (or the older `deployment.environment`), in lower case, `production` when left out, as the tracker's environments are ([environments and releases](web-analytics.md#environments-and-releases)), so a release's errors, traces, and logs line up. Attributes are kept as text in maps (`Map(LowCardinality(String), String)`): strings as they are, numbers and booleans as written, arrays and nested values as JSON. A bloom filter finds a trace's spans and logs by trace ID, and a text index (ClickHouse's `text` index, in 26.8) finds logs by the words in their body.

## Limits

What one project sends must not slow the API for the others, so every limit bounds the work a request makes, not only its size.

- **Requests:** 16 MiB once unpacked (the specification suggests 64 MiB as a ceiling). At most a million messages, the records, attributes, values, and what holds them, with each element of a list of numbers (a histogram's bounds and counts), which the API counts from the body before decoding it: decoding allocates for each, and an empty one takes two bytes, so 16 MiB of them would take gigabytes of memory. A million take about as long and as much memory as a large real batch (20,000 spans of 20 attributes: 120 ms and 120 MB on the development machine). Each API process works on at most 8 requests at once, at most 4 of them one project's, so one project's exporters cannot take every place: each holds its body in memory until ClickHouse has it.
- **Budgets:** per project and per instance, apart from events', so telemetry cannot crowd out a site's events: a burst of 50,000 a project, then 5,000 a second; the instance's three times that (150,000, then 15,000 a second), all projects together. A request costs one per record, or, when it is more, one per 64 messages it decodes into or per 2 KiB its rows hold (its body, and the resource attributes each row repeats), so a few records with many attributes, or with a large resource, cost what the work they make costs. Each exception that becomes an error event ([below](#exceptions-join-the-error-list)) costs one more, so a span's exceptions cannot multiply rows for free, and so does each span that is an AI call, which is written to `ai_calls` too ([AI usage](ai-usage.md#storage)), up to the whole budget: AI calls never make a request too large (413), so a batch that fits without them waits for the whole budget (429) instead. AI calls sent to `POST /api/ai-calls` spend the same budget, one each ([AI usage](ai-usage.md#sending-calls-over-http)). Decoding is paid for before it is done, and kept when the request is then refused. A first guess, to be measured.
- **Batches:** a request that costs more than a project's whole budget is refused (413), as it could never fit. A Collector's batch processor sends 8,192 records at a time by default, but with no upper limit (`send_batch_max_size: 0`), so one large incoming batch can pass through whole: set `send_batch_max_size` to 50,000 or less.
- **Records:** an all-zero trace, span, or parent span ID (a log record's, a span's parent, a link's) is stored as none, as OTLP says it means, so such a span is a root. Labels (service, scope name and version, severity text, event name, unit) lose control characters before they are cut to 256 characters. Attribute values cut to 4 KB, at most 128 attributes a record (the rest dropped, and counted, as OTLP counts its own dropped attributes), log bodies cut to 64 KB, at most 128 events and links a span. A resource's attributes are kept in key order up to 8 KiB in all, since each of its records' rows carries a copy; its service, release, and environment are read from all of them. Times outside the retention, or more than an hour ahead, are refused and counted in `partial_success`, and so are histogram points with more than 64 bounds (a chart's memory grows with the buckets; OpenTelemetry's default has 15; checked before any bound is read) or a count or bucket count of 2^63 or more (a chart takes differences as signed 64-bit numbers).
- **Retention:** telemetry is kept 30 days by default (`TELEMETRY_RETENTION_DAYS`, 1 to 365; decided on 18 November 2026, as long as session replays), dropped by whole days by ClickHouse itself (TTL), which costs nothing. Deleting a project deletes its telemetry, through `project_deletions` as for events.

## AI prompts and completions are not stored

Decided on 22 November 2026: for every project, always, with no setting to turn it off. AI instrumentations can record a model call's prompt and output as attributes, and three common ones do it unless told not to: the Vercel AI SDK ("By default, both inputs and outputs are recorded"), OpenLLMetry (unless `TRACELOOP_TRACE_CONTENT=false`), and OpenInference (`OPENINFERENCE_HIDE_INPUTS` and `OPENINFERENCE_HIDE_OUTPUTS` default to false). That text is whatever people typed and models answered, and anyone with the monitoring area could read it for 30 days. So the API drops it while it normalizes a request, before anything is written.

- **Where:** the attributes of spans, span events, span links, and log records; and the body of a log record that is a GenAI message event (`gen_ai.system.message`, `gen_ai.user.message`, `gen_ai.assistant.message`, `gen_ai.tool.message`, `gen_ai.choice`, `gen_ai.client.inference.operation.details`), named in the record's event name or, from older SDKs, in its `event.name` attribute. Resource and metric attributes are left as they are: none of the conventions puts content there.
- **How:** by the attribute's name alone, from one list (`apps/api/src/telemetry/ai-content.ts`): exact names, names dropped with everything under them (`llm.input_messages` drops `llm.input_messages.0.message.content`), and OpenLLMetry's numbered names (`gen_ai.prompt.0.content`, but not `gen_ai.prompt.name`). Each check is a set lookup; nothing else is read.
- **Counted:** each dropped attribute, and a dropped body, adds one to the span's or log record's dropped attributes count, and takes none of its 128 places. Span events and links keep no count of their own, so what they lose is not counted.
- **Shown:** a span or log record with AI attributes (`gen_ai.`, `ai.`, `llm.`, `traceloop.`, `openinference.`) says, under its attributes or its body, "AI prompts and completions are removed before storing."

## Exceptions join the error list

A server's exception is recorded on its span, as an event named `exception`, or, as OpenTelemetry now leans to, as a log record with `exception.type`, `exception.message`, and `exception.stacktrace` ([semantic conventions](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/exceptions/exceptions-logs/)). Ottertrail turns each into an error event ([errors](errors.md#server-errors)): a row of the events table named `error`, grouped with the same fingerprint rule, in the same list as the browser's.

- **What becomes an error:** a span event named `exception` with `exception.type` or `exception.message`, at most the first 10 of a span, among the 128 events its row keeps; and a log record with `exception.type` or `exception.message` among its attributes. Only records that are stored make errors: a refused span or log record makes none.
- **Type, message, and stack:** `exception.type`, or `Error` when only a message is given; `exception.message`; `exception.stacktrace`. Each is cut to what a browser's error may hold (type 200 characters, message 2,000, stack 16,000), without the control characters a browser's may not have (tabs and line feeds stay in the message and stack). They are read from the record as sent, not from its row, whose attribute values are cut to 4 KB; each is cut to four times its limit before it is cleaned, so a huge value costs no more than a large one.
- **Handled:** an exception on a span counts as handled unless the span's status is `ERROR`. One in a log record counts as handled below the error severity (`severity_number` 1 to 16), and as not handled from it (17 to 24) or without a severity.
- **Time:** the span event's time when it falls within the span (up to a minute after its end), else the span's start, decided from the span alone so every attempt gives the same time; the log record's time (its arrival's when it has none). Servers' clocks are trusted, as server-side events' are: no correction.
- **Environment, release, and service:** the resource's, as the telemetry rows have them ([storage](#storage)), so a release's errors, traces, and logs line up.
- **Path:** the span's `http.route`; for a log record, its own `http.route` if it has one; otherwise none. Cut to 2,048 characters, a browser's path's limit.
- **User:** the record's `user.id`, or the older `enduser.id`, when it is an ID a browser could send (1 to 200 characters, no control characters, [known users](web-analytics.md#known-users)); otherwise none. A server's user ID is not signed: it comes with the project's secret key.
- **Trace and span:** three columns of the events table, `trace_id`, `span_id` (in hex, as the spans table has them), and `service`, empty for every other event. A log record without a trace leaves them empty but the service. No visitor, browser, operating system, device, or location: a server has none.
- **Retries:** each error event's ID is made from a SHA-256 of the project and the exception's identity (a span's: its trace ID, span ID, the event's place among the span's events, its time as sent, type, and message; a log record's: its resource, its times as sent, and its content, so a record with no time keeps its ID even though it takes its arrival's time), as a UUID with version 8. A retried request makes the same IDs, and two alike exceptions on one span stay two, and the events table keeps each once ([ClickHouse](clickhouse.md)). The route writes the telemetry rows and the error events before it answers 200; if either insert fails, the budget is given back and the answer is 503, and the retry writes both again.
- **Grouping:** the fingerprint reads the file of the frame where the exception was raised from Python, Ruby, Go, Java (and other JVM languages; the class, not the file), and .NET stacks as well as browsers' and Node.js's ([errors](errors.md#3-grouping)).
- **Kept** as long as the project's events, not the 30 days of telemetry: an error can outlive the trace it names.

The error's page shows the latest occurrence's service, trace ID, and span ID. The trace ID, there and in each server occurrence's row, opens the trace's page on the span the exception happened in, looked for a day either side of the occurrence ([below](#how-the-pages-read)). Errors are kept longer than traces, so the page may say the trace is no longer kept.

## The dashboard

- **Traces** (built): a project's recent traces, by their root span: service, name, duration, status, filtered by service, environment, name, errors, duration, and period, under a chart of the traces per time bucket, those with errors apart, or their 95th-percentile duration ([below](#the-lists-charts)). A trace's page shows its spans as a waterfall, each with its attributes and events, and the trace's logs below them.
- **Logs** (built): search by period, service, environment, severity, words in the body, and trace; newest first; each record with its attributes, and its trace when it has one; a chart of the records per time bucket by severity ([below](#the-lists-charts)); and a live view of the records arriving now ([below](#live)).
- **Metrics** (built): each metric by name, with its kind, unit, services, when it was last seen, and a sparkline of the period; a metric's page charts it over the period: a gauge's average, minimum, and maximum; a counter's rate per second; a histogram's percentiles from its buckets and its observations per second; split by one attribute ([below](#how-metrics-are-charted)).
- **Services** (built): a chart of every service's requests over the period (per second by status class, the failed share, or the 95th percentile); for each service, and each route of its server spans: requests, the share that failed, the median, 95th, and 99th-percentile duration, and the requests by status class, over the period; a service's or route's page charts them over the period ([below](#how-services-are-counted)).
- **Databases** (built): a chart of every database call over the period (calls per second, or the time spent); the database calls inside traces, by database and query shape: calls, calls per second, time spent, the median, 95th, and 99th-percentile duration, and the failed share over the period; a shape's page charts them, lists its slowest calls with links to their spans, and the services and routes that make them ([below](#databases)).
- **Hosts** (built): the servers an OpenTelemetry Collector reports on, by host name, with their latest CPU, memory, fullest filesystem (each with a bar out of 100%), and load, and each host's charts over the period ([below](#hosts)).

### How the pages read

Twenty routes, beside the other project routes (`apps/api/src/telemetry/dashboard-routes.ts`, queries in `traces.ts`, `logs.ts`, `metrics.ts`, `services.ts`, `databases.ts`, `hosts.ts`, and `dashboard-query.ts`). Each runs the access check every project route runs and reads only the project it found; an unknown project gets 404. Every query filters by project first, then time (a metric's, by project, then name, then time), passes every value as a query parameter, and has a `LIMIT`. Each runs with the same settings (`DASHBOARD_SETTINGS`): it stops after 15 seconds (`max_execution_time`) or 300 MB (`max_memory_usage`), and either answers 503, saying to narrow the range or filters; a grouping or sort past 100 MB goes on in parts on disk first (`max_bytes_before_external_group_by`, `max_bytes_before_external_sort`). A request's queries end once it is answered or closed, whichever comes first: when the browser gives up, and when one query fails and the route answers with an error while the request's other queries still run or wait. The route passes an abort signal to the ClickHouse client, which closes the query's connection, and `cancel_http_readonly_queries_on_client_close` has ClickHouse stop the query. That setting acts only on a read-only query, and the client sends its queries as POST with `readonly` 0 unless told otherwise, so the dashboard's queries run with `readonly` 2 (read-only, settings still changeable; a grouping or sort spilled to disk is still a read). Without it, a query whose request was gone ran on to its 15-second limit (`telemetry-dashboard-limit.test.ts` checks that an aborted query leaves `system.processes` within a second). At most 4 dashboard queries run at once on each API process (`dashboard-limit.ts`), at most 2 of one request's, at most 1 of the Metrics list's sparklines, all of them together, and at most 1 of the Logs page's live polls, all of them together ([live](#live)), so a page that makes many queries, such as a host's, leaves places for the other pages; the next waits for a place, and after 10 seconds answers 503 with `Retry-After`, so the dashboard's pages cannot fill ClickHouse. The dashboard does not retry a telemetry request the API answered, a 503 included, which would only add to the load: the page says it failed and offers Retry. A request that never reached the API is retried once. The pages are described in the [dashboard](dashboard.md#89-traces-and-logs).

A retried record may be stored twice until ClickHouse merges its day ([storage](#storage)), so what counts or shows records must not see a copy, with one exception: the pages and alert rules that add up large windows of spans or log records (the Services page, a service's and route's charts, route alert rules, the Databases pages, and the Traces and Logs charts) read without `FINAL`, since `FINAL` was measured to fail past the 300 MB a query may use on a busy project's unmerged parts of spans ([how services are counted](#how-services-are-counted)). There a copy counts twice until its day is merged, which the tables force within about an hour of the day's last insert. Queries that read a few rows found by a key or an index read `FINAL`: a trace's spans and logs, a span, a log record, and each listed trace's span count. The two lists do not: `FINAL` merges every row of the range before the first can be sent, so each page of the Logs list read every record of the period (410,283 of 410,283 rows, on 400,000 records over 8 days). Without it, ClickHouse reads the project's rows in key order, newest first (`Read type: InReverseOrder` in `EXPLAIN actions = 1`), and stops at the `LIMIT`: 82,603 rows for the same page, about one block per day's part, however many records the period holds; the traces list read 23,168 of 400,000 root spans instead of all of them. A list fetches twice its page and one more row, and drops a row with the same time and ID as the row before it: copies of one record share those, so they sit next to each other in that order. The cursor is the last row's time and ID, as before. The environments and services lists, and "has the project sent anything", read no `FINAL` either: copies do not change them. The lists' charts read without `FINAL` too ([below](#the-lists-charts)), as the Services page does: each adds up every row of the period, so a retried record or root span counts twice until its day is merged. The live view reads without `FINAL` and drops copies by ID ([live](#live)).

- **Time:** the days asked for, in the instance's timezone, as on the other pages (at most 366), cut to the retention: nothing older than `TELEMETRY_RETENTION_DAYS` is read.
- **The traces list:** root spans (no parent) of the range and environment, newest first, 100 a page; the next page starts below the last row's time and ID (`before`, a cursor the API gives as `next`). A name filter is a part of the root's name, any case; a minimum duration is the root's. "With errors" keeps traces with a failed span in the range or an hour either side, from the latest 10,000 failed spans there, so the set ClickHouse builds for the filter stays bounded; with more failures than that in a period, a shorter period finds the older ones. Each row's span count and Error badge come from a second query for the page's trace IDs, in the same window (the range, an hour either side), so the badge and the filter agree. A trace whose root never arrived is not listed; a trace with two roots is listed twice. The environments and services to choose from come from the range's root spans, grouped (1,000 pairs at most).
- **A trace:** its spans by trace ID, which the bloom filter on `trace_id` finds (on a table of 3 million spans, it read 4 of 381 granules), within the retention, or a day either side of `at` when the page knows when the trace ran (an error's occurrence, the list's start time). At most 2,000 spans, by start; past that, a count of all of them says how many the page leaves out. Its log records the same way, at most 500, oldest first, as summaries (below). A trace with nothing kept answers with no spans, not an error, and the page says it may be past the retention.
- **A span:** found by its row's key (project, start, and ID), so its attributes, events, links, and resource attributes are read only when it is chosen: a trace's page lists only what the waterfall needs.
- **Logs:** newest first, 50 a page, by cursor as the traces list. A list sends a summary of each record: its ID, time, severity, service, environment, trace and span, event name, and the first 1,000 characters of its body, cut by ClickHouse (`substringUTF8`), with `bodyTruncated` when there is more. A record may hold a 64 KB body, 128 attributes of 4 KB, and 8 KiB of resource attributes, so whole records would make a page of 50 about 30 MB, and a trace's 500 about 290 MB; a summary is about 1 KB. Opening a record reads it whole (`/logs/:logId?time=`), found by its row's key (project, time, and ID), as a span is. A minimum severity is a severity number: Info and above is 9 and up; records with no severity (0) are left out once one is chosen. A trace ID uses the bloom filter. Words use the text index on `body`: the search is cut into words as the index cuts bodies (`splitByNonAlpha`: ASCII letters and digits, and anything beyond ASCII, so `card_declined` is the two words `card` and `declined`), at most 8, and each must be in the body, in any case, whole or as part of a longer word (`body ILIKE '%word%'` per word, all of them). A word never holds `%`, `_`, or a backslash, which separate words. In ClickHouse 26.8, `hasToken`, `hasAnyTokens`, `hasAllTokens`, `LIKE '%word%'`, and `ILIKE '%word%'` read the index: on a table of 2 million records with a word in 11 of them, `EXPLAIN indexes = 1` showed `body_text` keeping 1 of 254 granules for each, with query parameters and `FINAL` too. `hasTokenCaseInsensitive` reads every body, and so does a pattern of two words with a space between them, which is why each word is its own condition. A test (`telemetry-dashboard.test.ts`) checks that the plan still names the index, so an upgrade that loses it fails.

### The lists' charts

Above the Traces and Logs lists, a chart of the period with the list's filters ([dashboard](dashboard.md#the-lists-charts)). Each is one query (`traceChart` in `traces.ts`, `logVolume` in `logs.ts`), behind the same access check as its list (`monitoring` at view), with the same settings and the per-query time limit, and a `LIMIT` of its buckets.

- **Buckets** (`listChartBuckets` in `dashboard-query.ts`): the shortest length of 1, 2, 5, 10, 15, or 30 minutes, 1, 2, 3, 6, or 12 hours, or 1, 2, 3, or 4 days, or a week, that keeps them within 120 over the range's span on the local clock, up to the end of the range or now, whichever is sooner. As for the metric charts ([below](#how-metrics-are-charted)), a range of several days takes its step from all of it, so a 3-day range is hourly at 00:05 as at 23:55, and only today's follows the part that has passed (`telemetry-dashboard.test.ts`). Each bucket starts on the clock of the instance's timezone: a length under a day on the day's marks (00:00, 06:00, 12:00, and 18:00 for 6 hours), whatever the date's offset from UTC, and a length of days on a midnight, counted from the range's first day. So a daylight saving change makes one bucket an hour longer or shorter instead of moving every later bucket off the clock, and a day's bucket is that day (`telemetry-dashboard.test.ts` checks Europe/Berlin across 25 October 2026 and America/New_York across 1 November 2026). The answer lists each bucket's start (`bucketStarts`). A mark the clock skips (02:00 on a change forward, or midnight in a zone such as America/Santiago that skips it) starts at the first moment that exists after it, and a slot the clock skips whole (02:00 to 03:00 with a 1-hour step) has no bucket, so one bucket is shorter. A mark the clock repeats (02:00 to 02:59 on a change back) starts at its first occurrence, so the bucket that holds the repeated hour is an hour longer (02:55 to 03:00 is 65 minutes with a 5-minute step): one bar, never two for one wall-clock time. ClickHouse places each row by its moment, not its wall clock: the starts go to the query as an array of milliseconds, and a row's bucket is the last start at or before it (`arrayCount`), so no row falls outside a bucket and none counts twice, and the bars add up to the total. `telemetry-dashboard.test.ts` places every minute of America/New_York's 8 March 2026 (2-hour, 1-hour, and 15-minute steps), America/Santiago's 6 September 2026 (6 hours), and Europe/Berlin's 25 October 2026 (5, 10, and 15 minutes), each in the bucket whose span holds it. Today at noon is 72 buckets of 10 minutes; a past day, 96 of 15 minutes; 7 days, about 80 of 2 hours; 30 days, 120 of 6 hours; 90 days, 90 of a day; 12 months, about 92 of 4 days. A row later than the last bucket (a clock ahead) counts in the last. Fewer buckets than the Metrics page's 300 keep each bar a few pixels wide in a list's pane.
- **Records** (`GET …/logs/volume`): the records the search would find (the project first, then the range, the environment, the service, the minimum severity, the words, and the trace ID, as the list's own conditions), grouped by bucket and severity range: none (0), trace (1 to 4), debug, info, warn, error, and fatal (21 to 24). It reads without `FINAL`, as the Traces chart and the Services page do: a count reads every row of the range, the read `FINAL` was measured to fail on for spans, so a retried record counts twice until its day is merged, which the logs table forces within about an hour of the day's last insert (`telemetry-dashboard.test.ts` sends a record twice with merges stopped and counts it twice; the list shows it once).
- **Traces** (`GET …/traces/chart`): the list's root spans (no parent), with the list's filters, grouped by bucket: how many, how many with errors, and the exact 95th percentile of their durations (`quantileExactInclusive`). A trace has errors exactly as "With errors only" finds it: its trace ID is among the latest 10,000 failed spans (the root's own failure included) of the range and an hour either side. When the window holds more failed spans than that, older traces with errors count as without, and the answer says so (`failedCapped`), which the page shows under the title. It reads without `FINAL`, as the Services page and its charts do: it adds up every root span of the period, the read `FINAL` was measured to fail on, so a retried root counts twice until its day is merged.

### Live

The Logs page's live view ([dashboard](dashboard.md#live)) asks every 2 seconds for the records that arrived since its last poll: `GET …/logs/live`, a route of its own beside the search, behind the same access check (`monitoring` at view), with the search's filters and no period.

- **Arrival:** each log record has `received_at`, ClickHouse's clock as it writes the record (`DEFAULT now64(9)`, migration 0005), with a minmax index. The view goes by arrival, not by when the record happened, so a record sent late with an old time still shows. Records stored before the migration took their observed time.
- **The cursor** (`after`) is an arrival, in nanoseconds since 1970: the moment the last poll read up to. Each poll reads up to 2 seconds before ClickHouse's now (`LIVE_SETTLE_MS`), so a batch still being committed is not passed, and answers that moment as the next cursor. Each poll also reads again the 10 seconds before its cursor (`LIVE_REREAD_MS`), so a part that became visible later than 2 seconds after its rows' arrival (a slow commit, an index being built) is still found; the page drops the records it already holds, by ID. A part later than that can be missed.
- **Skipping ahead:** a poll sends the newest 200 records (`LIVE_PAGE_SIZE`), newest first, and counts the records that arrived after its cursor (a scalar count of their distinct IDs in the same query, so a retried record's copies count once). When more than a page arrived, the older ones are skipped and the answer says how many (`skipped`); the page marks them. A backlog is never paged through, so a burst cannot start a run of polls one after another.
- **Gaps:** a cursor older than the window of the last 5 minutes (`LIVE_WINDOW_MS`), after a stop or a tab hidden that long, starts again from the window's start, and the answer gives what it left out (`gap`, from the cursor to the window's start, or to the oldest record sent when the window held more than a page). Without a cursor, a poll starts from the window, which is no gap.
- **Bounds:** the project first, then the arrival (from the cursor less the re-read, or the window's start, to the settled now), and records no older than the retention by their own time; 200 a poll, with the per-query time limit. Two reads a poll: ClickHouse's clock, and the records with their count.
- **Its own limit:** live polls run behind a pool of their own (`liveQueryLimiter` in `dashboard-limit.ts`, taken first, as the sparklines' is, through `dashboardReader`'s `share`): one at a time on each API process (`LIVE_QUERIES_AT_ONCE`), each then taking one of the dashboard's 4 places, so polls from every page watching hold at most one of them. With the sparklines' one (`SPARKLINE_PLACES`), the 4 places split as: at most 1 for sparklines, at most 1 for live polls, and at least 2 always left for the lists, the charts, and the Overview (`LIST_PLACES`). A poll waits at most 2 seconds for its live place (`LIVE_QUEUE_WAIT_MS`), and at most as long again for one of the dashboard's places once it holds it (`liveReader`'s `mainWaitMs`), never the 10 seconds other pages wait; refused either time, it answers 503, and the page asks again at its next poll without a message (`telemetry-dashboard-limit.test.ts`).
- **Copies:** read without `FINAL`. A retry is written again with a later arrival, so the live view would see it twice; a poll drops a second row with the same ID, and the page drops a record whose ID it already holds.
- **The migration** (0005) is safe to run again after a failure part way: it sets the default to the observed time again before `MATERIALIZE COLUMN`, and to `now64(9)` last. On ClickHouse 26.8.38, `MATERIALIZE COLUMN` writes the column only into parts that lack it and leaves values already stored as they are (checked in a scratch database: a row's arrival written at insert, and one given explicitly, kept their values through `MATERIALIZE COLUMN` with another default). Without the reset, a rerun after the switch to `now64(9)` would write the rerun's time into parts still without the column. `clickhouse.test.ts` runs the migration's statements a second time and finds every arrival unchanged.

### How metrics are charted

- **The list:** the metrics with points in the range and environment, from both metric tables, grouped by name and kind, by name, at most 1,000 (the answer says when there were more). A kind is what the dashboard calls each: a gauge; a counter (a monotonic sum); an up-down counter (a sum that is not monotonic); a histogram (explicit buckets). A name sent as two kinds is listed twice. Each has its latest unit, up to 10 of its services and how many there are, and its latest point's time. Read without `FINAL`: copies change none of these.
- **A series** is one value of the `series` column ([storage](#storage)). A chart reads at most 1,000 series, those with the most points in the range, found by a first query; the answer gives how many it read and whether there were more. The metric's name is a query parameter of at most 1,024 bytes, as ingestion stores it; a split key at most 256 characters.
- **Time buckets:** from the start of the range's first day in the instance's timezone, of the shortest length of 1, 2, 5, 10, 15, or 30 minutes, 1, 2, 3, 6, or 12 hours, 1 or 2 days, or a week that keeps them within 300 (30 for a sparkline, below), up to the end of the range or now, whichever is sooner. A range of several days takes its step from all of it, to its end, not from the part that has passed, so its chart keeps one step at every time of day: 7 days are hourly at 00:10 as at 23:50 (counting only the part up to now, they were 30 minutes until 06:00). Today's chart takes its step from the part up to now, so its first hours are not a few wide buckets: minutes just after midnight, 5 minutes from 10:00. A day is 288 buckets of 5 minutes; 7 days, hours; 30 days, 3 hours. `telemetry-metrics.test.ts` checks each period's step from just after midnight to just before the next. Points are read `FINAL`, so a retried point counts once, from an hour (or a bucket, if longer) before the range, so a cumulative series' first point in the range has the one before it.
- **Gauges:** the average, minimum, and maximum of the points in each bucket.
- **Up-down counters:** each series' last value in each bucket, added up: how many connections were open, across all the processes. A series that sent nothing in a bucket adds nothing to it. A delta series sends changes, never its level, so it adds its points in the bucket instead: +5 and -2 add 3. Its line is then the net change per bucket, in the chart, the sparkline (whose buckets are longer, so they add more changes), and a metric rule's window, where a change counts wherever it falls in the window (a cumulative series counts only while its last point is fresh). OpenTelemetry's SDKs and the Collector's host metrics send up-down counters cumulative, so this is rare; the hosts list reads each series' latest value as before (`telemetry-metrics.test.ts` checks both temporalities).
- **Counters:** a rate per second. ClickHouse orders each series' points by time (`lagInFrame`), apart for each start time: two processes that share a series (the same attributes, no `service.instance.id`) but started at different times are two runs, so their values do not mix. A cumulative point's increase is its value less the previous point's. A value below the previous one is a reset: the new value is the increase. A run's first point counts from its start time when that is known and within what was read; otherwise it is only the base for the next. Each increase is spread over the buckets its interval (from the previous point, or the start, to the point) covers, in proportion to the overlap, at most the chart's buckets, so a process that exports every 15 minutes counts in each 5-minute bucket, not a third of them. A delta point's value counts in its own bucket. A bucket's rate is its increases over its seconds (the first and last cut to the range and now). Unspecified temporality is read as cumulative.
- **Histograms:** the observations per second are worked out from the count as a counter's rate, per point, with every series. The percentiles come from the bucket counts of the series with the bounds that have the most points: each series' counts per bucket are its last point's in the bucket (cumulative) or all its points' added up (delta), and a cumulative series' bucket counts less the bucket before's (all of them when any went down, or the run started then). The window that takes these differences sorts one row per series and bucket rather than per point, so its memory does not grow with the points; a reset inside a bucket counts only the counts since it. A bucket's counts are added up, then the 50th, 95th, and 99th percentiles are interpolated: the rank `q × total` falls in one bucket, and the value is that far along a straight line between the bucket's lower and upper bound. The first bucket starts at the minimum when known (else 0, or its bound when that is not above 0); the last, which has no upper bound, ends at the maximum when known, else at its lower bound. A cumulative point's minimum and maximum are those since its start, so they can be wider than the bucket's. Series with other bounds, or without bucket counts, are left out of the percentiles and counted in the answer; a histogram whose points carry no bucket counts has a rate and no percentiles.
- **Split by an attribute:** one line per value of an attribute key the metric's series have (the chart offers up to 100, by name), with points without it as one line of their own. Lines rank by volume over the whole range: points for gauges and up-down counters, the increase for counters, observations for histograms. With more than 8 values, the 7 with the most volume keep their lines and the rest are added up as one "other" line, in ClickHouse (`dense_rank` over the values), so a chart sends at most 8 lines of 300 values. A gauge's other line is the average, minimum, and maximum of all their points.
- **Sparklines** (`metricSparkline`, `GET /api/projects/:id/metrics/sparkline?name=…&kind=…`): one listed metric as its chart reads it, without a split, in at most 30 buckets of the same lengths (a day is 24 hours, 7 days 28 buckets of 6 hours), with the line its chart opens on: a gauge's average, an up-down counter's total, a counter's rate per second, a histogram's 95th percentile. A histogram's 95th percentile, not its observations per second, since its chart opens on the percentiles; one whose points carry no bucket counts answers `percentiles: false` and no values, and the page shows a dash. Two queries, as a chart's: the first ranks the series by their points (without `FINAL`), the second reads those series' points, `FINAL`, from the lookback before the range. A sparkline reads at most 100 series (`SPARKLINE_SERIES`; a chart reads 1,000), those with the most points, and the answer says when it left some out (`seriesCapped`); a histogram's reads only its bucket counts, not the counts its observations per second come from. Each reads only its metric's rows (project, then name, then time). The name is at most 1,024 bytes, as for a chart. A period longer than 7 days (`SPARKLINE_MAX_DAYS`) is refused with 400 (`range_too_long`): both reads take the metric's rows of the whole period, a list asks for one per row, and a 30-day line of a metric with thousands of series exported every minute would read more than a hundred million rows twice.
- **Their share of the places.** All sparkline requests of an API process together hold at most 1 of the 4 places (`SPARKLINE_PLACES`, a pool of its own the route's queries take a place of first, `dashboardReader`'s `share`), so the lists, the charts, and the Overview never wait behind a page of sparklines; a sparkline that waits 10 seconds for its turn answers 503, and its row shows a dash.
- **Lazy, not batched.** The Metrics page asks for a row's sparkline only while the row is within 200 pixels of the scrolling content panel's view (`app-content`, the observer's root): a row scrolled past does not ask again when the period or the environment changes, only once it is back in view. A page has at most 2 sparkline requests in flight (`SPARKLINES_AT_ONCE`); the rest wait their turn in the browser, and leave the queue when their row's query is dropped. One query for every listed metric was the other choice: it would read the listed metrics' rows once, but a counter's rate and a histogram's percentiles are worked out per series with window functions (`chartIn`), and grouping them by metric too would make a second implementation of every kind's chart, where a row's sparkline now reads exactly as its chart does.
- **Sparklines measured** with the dashboard's settings, `system.query_log`'s `read_rows` and `query_duration_ms` for the two queries, then the whole request:
  - The demo seed (`vp run seed`: 14 metrics, 55,519 points and 8,072 histogram points over 7 days), 7 days: an app's gauge, counter, or up-down counter read 6,692 and 6,692 to 7,063 rows (8 to 66 ms each; 27 to 99 ms in all); a host metric 45,936 and 45,936 to 48,456 rows (16 to 101 ms; 47 to 166 ms); a histogram 7,648 and 8,072 rows (7 to 36 ms; 56 to 58 ms). Before the cap and the two reads (one query that also counted the series, and a histogram's rate), the same lines read 19,992 to 139,896 rows each.
  - A larger project in a scratch database (dropped after): a gauge and a counter of 3,000 series each and a histogram of 1,000 series with 15 buckets, a point every 10 minutes for 7 days (3,024,000, 3,024,000, and 1,008,000 rows). Today: 270,000 and 270,000 rows for the gauge (61 and 43 ms; 148 ms in all), 270,000 and 292,400 for the counter (185 ms), 90,000 and 102,928 for the histogram (121 ms). 7 days: the gauge 2,870,209 rows twice (196 and 305 ms; 536 ms), the counter 2,862,000 and 2,974,848 (495 ms, 13 MiB), the histogram 954,000 and 991,616 (440 ms, 11 MiB). The metric's own 7-day chart, for comparison: the gauge 390 ms, the counter 970 ms (55 MiB), the histogram 2.1 s (130 MiB). A sparkline reads as many rows as its chart, since every series' rows are read to rank them and the rows of the series kept are spread over the same granules; the cap bounds the work and memory after the read, not the read.
- **Cost,** measured on the development machine (ClickHouse 26.8, the 4 GB Podman machine, scratch databases dropped after), with the settings above:
  - A busy project: 200 metrics of 25 series each, a cumulative point a minute, one day (7.2 million rows). Ordered by time, one metric's chart read every row of the day, about 3.4 GiB, and a 30-day chart about 107 GiB, past the 15 seconds. Ordered by name, a chart reads its metric's granules only: 65,536 rows and 3 MiB to find the series (5 ms, 8 MiB of memory), 90,112 rows and 5.4 MiB for the chart (27 ms, 12 MiB); split by an attribute, 9.2 MiB read (31 ms, 16 MiB).
  - A cumulative histogram of 30 series with 64 bounds (65 buckets), a point a minute. Before the per-bucket counts, a 7-day chart passed 300 MB and failed. Now 7 days: 267,392 rows and 146 MiB to find the series (27 ms), 539,264 rows and 165 MiB for the chart (181 ms, 75 MiB of memory); 30 days (1.3 million points): 1,260,992 rows and 688 MiB (104 ms, 27 MiB), then 2,521,984 rows and 770 MiB for the chart (593 ms, 84 MiB); split by an attribute, 983 ms and 134 MiB. The chart reads the metric's rows twice, once for the rate and once for the counts.

### How services are counted

A request is a span of kind `SERVER`; its route is its `http.route` attribute, or its name when it has none. For each service, and each of its routes, the page counts the requests in the range and environment, the failed ones (status `ERROR`), the median, 95th, and 99th-percentile duration, interpolated between the two nearest durations (`quantilesExactInclusive`, as the Speed page's p75), and the requests by status class. Two queries (`listServices` in `services.ts`), each over the spans `serverSpans` reads:

1. The counts, by a hash of each route rather than its name, for each service and each route at once (`GROUPING SETS ((service), (service, route))`), keeping each service's 100 busiest routes (`LIMIT 101 BY service`), at most 200 services. A service's route count is `uniq`: exact up to 65,536 routes, then approximate.
2. The names and the exact percentiles of each service and of the routes listed only: every other route's spans fall in one group per service, so the percentiles keep a few hundred groups however many routes there are.

- **Status classes:** a request's status code is its `http.response.status_code` attribute, else the older `http.status_code` (the newer name wins when a span has both). Its class is the code's hundreds: 2xx, 3xx, 4xx, or 5xx. A request with neither attribute, or with a code that is not a whole number from 200 to 599 (such as 101 or "OK"), counts as "no status". The five classes add up to the requests. A class is a count, not a failure: a 404 is not failed unless its span's status is `ERROR`.
- **A service's or route's charts** (`serviceChart`, `GET /api/projects/:id/services/chart?service=…&route=…`): one query over the same spans, grouped by time bucket and over the whole range at once (`GROUPING SETS ((), (bucket))`). The buckets are a metric chart's (`bucketsOf`): 5 minutes for a day, an hour for 7 days, 3 hours for 30 days. Each bucket has its requests and failed ones, each class's requests, and the exact median, 95th, and 99th percentile of its durations. The API gives each class's requests per second (its count over the bucket's seconds within the range and before now) and the failed share per bucket; a bucket without a request is 0 per second, and a gap in the failed share and the durations. A span later than the last bucket (a clock ahead of the server's) counts in the last, so the buckets add up to the totals. The totals come from the same read and are the Services page's numbers for the same range and environment: `telemetry-services.test.ts` checks both. A service name is a query parameter of at most 256 characters, a route of at most 4 KB, as ingestion stores them.
- **Every service's charts** (`servicesTrend`, `GET /api/projects/:id/services/trend`): the same query as a service's charts, without a service: every server span of the project's range and environment, in the same buckets (an hour for 7 days, at most 300), with each bucket's requests, failed ones, each class's requests, and percentiles, and the totals, in one read, `LIMIT` the buckets and one. The totals are the Services page's services added up while the list holds every service (200 at most; past that, the totals count every service, the list the first 200 by name), and the buckets add up to them. The Services page draws the requests per second by class, the failed share, and the 95th percentile. Measured on the demo seed (37,619 spans over 14 days): 7 days read 17,887 rows (the same as each of the Services page's queries) in 7 ms with 5 MiB of memory. Its memory is a service's chart's: the exact percentiles keep each bucket's durations, 38 MiB for 4.8 million server spans in the measurements below.

## Databases

The database calls inside traces: "which query got slow". Most OpenTelemetry instrumentations of database clients record each query as a span; Ottertrail groups those spans by database and query shape. Nothing more is sent: ClickHouse works out each call's database and shape when its span is written, and the pages read those columns (`apps/api/src/telemetry/databases.ts`).

### What is a database call

A span of kind `CLIENT` with a database system attribute. OpenTelemetry's [semantic conventions for database client spans](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/db/database-spans/) (stable; read on 18 November 2026) renamed most of their attributes, and the [registry](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/registry/attributes/db/) lists the older names as deprecated; instrumentations still send both, so each value is read from the first of its names a span has:

| What       | Current name         | Older names read                                                                       |
| ---------- | -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| System     | `db.system.name`     | `db.system`                                                                            |
| Database   | `db.namespace`       | `db.name`, `db.redis.database_index`, `db.elasticsearch.cluster.name`                  |
| Operation  | `db.operation.name`  | `db.operation`                                                                         |
| Collection | `db.collection.name` | `db.sql.table`, `db.mongodb.collection`, `db.cassandra.table`, `db.cosmosdb.container` |
| Summary    | `db.query.summary`   | none                                                                                   |
| Query text | `db.query.text`      | `db.statement`                                                                         |

A span without a system is not a database call, nor is one of another kind: the conventions allow `INTERNAL` for an in-memory database, which is left out. A call failed when its span's status is `ERROR`, as a request does on the Services page.

### Grouping

Calls are grouped by system, database, and query shape. The shape is, from the first a call has:

1. its `db.query.summary`: the conventions' own low-cardinality name for the query, such as `SELECT orders`;
2. its query text, normalized (below);
3. its operation and collection, such as `find products`;
4. its span's name.

The shape is shown cut to 1,000 characters. Its ID is a `cityHash64` of its system, database, source, and whole shape (up to the 4,096 characters normalized), so two long queries that differ only past the cut (`… where u1_0.email=$1` and `… where u1_0.id=$1`) are two shapes, and a shape's page keeps its address from one period to the next. Two calls with the same shape from a different source (a summary `SELECT orders` and an operation `SELECT orders`) stay apart.

### Worked out when a span is written

ClickHouse works out each span's system, database, and shape when the span is written: five `MATERIALIZED` columns of `spans`, as a metric row's `series` is (migration `0003_database_call_columns`, built from `apps/api/src/telemetry/db-shape.ts`): `db_system` (empty for a span that is no database call), `db_namespace`, `db_shape_kind` (1 summary, 2 query text, 3 operation and collection or name; 0 for no database call), `db_shape` (as shown, cut to 1,000 characters), and `db_shape_id`. The migration also works them out for the rows already stored (`MATERIALIZE COLUMN`, waited for). The rules are part of the migration: a change to them is a new migration that redefines the columns and materializes them again.

The text is normalized only when no summary is sent: otherwise its input is empty, which costs nothing. No regular expression sits inside a branch (`if`, `multiIf`): with short-circuit evaluation, ClickHouse copies a lazily worked out branch's input, and when the shapes were still worked out on each read, 100,000 texts of 4 KB passed 300 MB that way. So the columns choose with `coalesce` and `nullIf`, and give a span that is no database call `''` and 0 by arithmetic. The reads now hold no regular expression and run with the dashboard's settings unchanged.

### Normalization

In ClickHouse, in the columns' expressions, in one implementation. The first 4,096 characters of the text (as many as ingestion keeps of a value: 4 KB) go through seven regular expressions, each replacing every match (`replaceRegexpAll`), then the result is trimmed:

1. String literals become `?`, and each comment is kept, with `?` after it, for step 2. One expression finds both, leftmost first, so a quote inside a comment (`-- don't`, a `traceparent='…'` tag) starts no string, and a `--` or `/*` inside a string starts no comment. A string runs from a quote to the next one, with `''` and a backslash before any character inside; an unclosed one runs to the end.
2. Comments, with step 1's `?`, become a space: `-- …` to the end of the line, `/* … */`, and an unclosed `/*` to the end.
3. Numbers become `?`: whole, decimal, with an exponent, or hexadecimal, but not digits inside a name (`table2`, `col_1`, `@p1`). A numbered placeholder's number goes too: `$1` becomes `$?`.
4. Each run of white space becomes one space.
5. A list of placeholders after `IN` becomes `IN (?)`, however long, and one cut off at the end of the text too. A placeholder is `?`, `$?`, `@name`, or `:name`.
6. The same after `in`: each keeps the case it was written in.
7. Rows of values after a first are dropped, when each holds only placeholders, function calls without nested parentheses (`now()`, `lower(?)`), `NULL`, `DEFAULT`, `TRUE`, or `FALSE`: `VALUES ($1, $2, now()), ($3, $4, now())` becomes `VALUES ($?, $?, now())`. A list of anything else stays: `SELECT sum(a), (b + c)` and `SELECT sum(a), (b - c)` are two shapes.

| Query text                                                 | Shape                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| `SELECT * FROM orders WHERE id = 42 AND name = 'O''Brien'` | `SELECT * FROM orders WHERE id = ? AND name = ?` |
| `select * from t where id in (1, 2, 3)`                    | `select * from t where id in (?)`                |
| `SELECT * FROM t WHERE id IN (@p0, @p1, @p2)`              | `SELECT * FROM t WHERE id IN (?)`                |
| `INSERT INTO t (a, b) VALUES (1, 'x'), (2, 'y')`           | `INSERT INTO t (a, b) VALUES (?, ?)`             |
| `/*traceparent='00-…'*/ SELECT a FROM t -- by id`          | `SELECT a FROM t`                                |
| `UPDATE notes SET body = '--' WHERE id = 1`                | `UPDATE notes SET body = ? WHERE id = ?`         |
| `SELECT col_1 FROM t3 WHERE a = $1`                        | `SELECT col_1 FROM t3 WHERE a = $?`              |

Known limits:

- **A backslash before a quote** is read as escaping it, as MySQL does unless `NO_BACKSLASH_ESCAPES` is set. Standard SQL strings (PostgreSQL's by default) end at the quote even after a backslash, so in `WHERE path = 'C:\' AND kind = 'x'` the string runs on to the next quote, and the shape is `WHERE path = ?x?`, from there on unlike the same query with a path that does not end in a backslash.
- **Double quotes are left alone:** they hold names in standard SQL, so a database that also takes double-quoted strings keeps those in the shape.
- **Case is kept:** `select` and `SELECT` are two shapes.

Instrumentations may already replace literals with `?` before sending, as the conventions ask of non-parameterized text; normalizing again changes nothing.

ClickHouse runs regular expressions with RE2, whose time is linear in the text, with no backtracking, so no text can make one slow; `telemetry-databases.test.ts` sends 5,000 texts of 4 KB built to stress each step (`(((`, quotes, backslashes, unclosed comments, endless IN lists and rows, digits, emoji, control characters) and one of 64 KB, and checks every shape is within 1,000 characters and the pages answer in time. Only step 1 has a capture group: with one, RE2 finds each match's groups with its slower engine. Step 1 with it took 3.4 seconds on one thread for the 3.6 million statements below, where comments and then strings, without groups, took 1.5, but cut a string holding `--` short; the numbers step took 6.2 seconds with a group and 2.8 without. Skipping a step when its text could not match (`position` first) saved nothing measurable, so every step runs on every text.

### The pages read

- **The list** (`listDatabases`): one read of the columns, `GROUPING SETS ((db_system, db_namespace), (db_system, db_namespace, db_shape_id))`, with each group's calls, failed calls, total time, exact median, 95th, and 99th percentile (`quantilesExactInclusive`, as the Services page), and up to 10 of its services (`groupUniqArray(10)`) with how many there are. Window functions then rank the databases by time spent and each database's shapes by the sort asked for (`sort=time`, the default; `p95`, `calls`, or `failures`), and keep 50 databases and 200 shapes of each; the answer says when either was cut. Calls per second are the calls over the period's seconds before now. A second query lists the environments, as the other pages do.
- **A shape's page** (`shapeDetail`): one read of the shape's calls (`PREWHERE db_shape_id = …`), `GROUPING SETS ((), (bucket), (service))`: the totals, which are the list's for the same period; each time bucket's calls, failed calls, and percentiles, in a metric chart's buckets (`bucketsOf`: an hour for 7 days), a call past the last bucket counted in the last so the buckets add up to the totals; each service's numbers, most calls first, at most 50; and the 20 slowest calls with their trace, span, start, service, and status (`groupArraySorted`, kept only in the totals' row). Then its routes: the 1,000 latest calls, read from the latest buckets that hold 1,000 (the whole range for a shape with fewer), and for each, the earliest server span of the same trace and service between an hour before the earliest of them and the latest, found by trace ID (the bloom filter); its route is the Services page's (`http.route`, else its name). A call with no such span (a background job's, or a request whose span never arrived) counts under no route.
- **Every database's charts** (`databasesTrend`, `GET /api/projects/:id/databases/trend`): every database call of the range and environment, in a metric chart's buckets (`bucketsOf`, at most 300), `GROUPING SETS ((), (bucket))`: each bucket's calls, failed calls, and time spent (the durations added up), and the totals, which are the Databases page's databases added up while the list holds every database (50 at most; past that, the totals count every database, the list the 50 with the most time spent). Only counts and sums, so the read holds one row per bucket, not each call's duration. A call past the last bucket counts in the last. Calls per second are a bucket's calls over its seconds within the range and before now. Measured on the demo seed: 7 days read 17,887 rows (as the list does) in 5 ms with 5 MiB of memory.
- **Without `FINAL`,** as the Services pages ([how services are counted](#how-services-are-counted)): a retried span counts twice until its day is merged, which the spans table forces within about an hour of the day's last insert.
- **`PREWHERE`** keeps the project's rows of the range that are database calls (`kind = 'client' AND db_system != ''`), and a shape's page's rows of the shape, before any other column is read. The latest calls are read without read-in-order: newest first over many unmerged parts, ClickHouse kept a block of each part in memory.
- Every query carries the dashboard's settings and the limit on queries at once, and ends when its request does ([how the pages read](#how-the-pages-read)). A shape ID is a 64-bit number in decimal; anything else is refused.

## Hosts

A server's own health (its CPU, memory, disks, network, and load) comes from an OpenTelemetry Collector running on it, with the Collector's host metrics receiver. The Collector sends these as ordinary OTLP metrics, which Ottertrail stores as any other ([storage](#storage)); the Metrics page can chart each one alone. The Hosts pages read them together, per host. Nothing of Ottertrail's own runs on the server.

### What the pages read

A host is the `host.name` attribute of the metrics' resource, which the Collector's resource detection processor sets ([below](#the-collector)). The host's name is matched as ingestion stores it: a value of at most 4 KB, any characters.

Only the receiver's metrics count. An app's SDK can send metrics with the same names under the same `host.name`: OpenTelemetry's Node.js SDK sets `host.name` with its host detector, and its `@opentelemetry/host-metrics` package sends `system.cpu.time`, `system.cpu.utilization`, and `system.memory.usage` for the app's machine. Counted as the host's, an app alone would be listed as a host, and an app beside a Collector would add its own series to the host's CPU and memory. So every query keeps only the series of an instrumentation scope whose name starts as the receiver's scrapers name theirs: `github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-collector-contrib/receiver/hostmetricsreceiver/` (now: the scraper's package, such as `…/internal/scraper/cpuscraper`), or `otelcol/hostmetricsreceiver/` (older releases, such as `otelcol/hostmetricsreceiver/cpu` in v0.80.0). Both were read in the receiver's generated code (`internal/metadata`) on 18 November 2026.

The names, units, and attributes are those of the host metrics receiver's scrapers and of OpenTelemetry's semantic conventions for system metrics, checked on 18 November 2026 (the receiver's [CPU](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-collector-contrib/blob/main/receiver/hostmetricsreceiver/internal/scraper/cpuscraper/documentation.md), [memory](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-collector-contrib/blob/main/receiver/hostmetricsreceiver/internal/scraper/memoryscraper/documentation.md), [filesystem](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-collector-contrib/blob/main/receiver/hostmetricsreceiver/internal/scraper/filesystemscraper/documentation.md), [network](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-collector-contrib/blob/main/receiver/hostmetricsreceiver/internal/scraper/networkscraper/documentation.md), [disk](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-collector-contrib/blob/main/receiver/hostmetricsreceiver/internal/scraper/diskscraper/documentation.md), and [load](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-collector-contrib/blob/main/receiver/hostmetricsreceiver/internal/scraper/loadscraper/documentation.md) scrapers; the [semantic conventions](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/system/system-metrics/), still in development):

| Metric                                                             | Kind and unit          | Attributes read                                                          | On by default |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------- |
| `system.cpu.time`                                                  | Counter, seconds       | `state` (`idle`, `user`, `system`, `wait`, …)                            | Yes           |
| `system.cpu.utilization`                                           | Gauge, 0 to 1          | `state`                                                                  | No            |
| `system.memory.usage`                                              | Up-down counter, bytes | `state` (`used`, `free`, `buffered`, `cached`, `slab_…`)                 | Yes           |
| `system.memory.utilization`                                        | Gauge, 0 to 1          | `state`                                                                  | No            |
| `system.filesystem.usage`                                          | Up-down counter, bytes | `mountpoint`, `mode`, `state` (`used`, `free`, `reserved`)               | Yes           |
| `system.filesystem.utilization`                                    | Gauge, 0 to 1          | `mountpoint`, `mode` (the receiver's has no state: it is the share used) | No            |
| `system.network.io`                                                | Counter, bytes         | `direction` (`receive`, `transmit`), every `device` added up             | Yes           |
| `system.disk.io`                                                   | Counter, bytes         | `direction` (`read`, `write`), every `device` added up                   | Yes           |
| `system.cpu.load_average.1m`, `system.cpu.load_average.5m`, `…15m` | Gauge, threads         | none                                                                     | Yes           |

The semantic conventions name some attributes differently from the receiver: `cpu.mode` for the CPU's state, `system.memory.state`, `system.filesystem.state`, `system.filesystem.mountpoint`, `system.filesystem.mode`, `network.io.direction`, and `disk.io.direction`; older conventions had `system.cpu.state`. The pages read any of them, the receiver's first, so a receiver that moves to the conventions' names keeps working. The conventions define no load average; the receiver's names are read.

The utilization metrics are off by default, so without them the shares are worked out from the default metrics, the fallbacks below. The configuration the page shows ([below](#the-collector)) turns them on, so the shares are exact; the pages prefer them when sent.

### How the values are worked out

Each chart reads its metrics as the Metrics page does ([above](#how-metrics-are-charted)): rates from cumulative sums with resets and restarts, a gauge's average per bucket, an up-down counter's last value per series and bucket added up. Every chart query also filters by the host (`resource_attributes['host.name']` equal to a query parameter) and the receiver's scopes, and splits by the first of the attribute's names a point has. Shares are percentages from 0 to 100.

- **CPU busy:** the share of CPU time not in the `idle` state, all CPUs together. From `system.cpu.utilization` when the host sends it: per bucket, each state's average, and the busy share is all states but idle over all states. Otherwise from `system.cpu.time`: each state's time per second (its rate, all CPUs added up), and the busy share is the same ratio of rates. `wait` (time waiting for I/O) counts as busy: it is not idle. The page also charts each state's share.
- **Memory used:** from `system.memory.utilization`'s `used` state when sent. Otherwise `system.memory.usage`'s `used` over the sum of its states but `slab_reclaimable` and `slab_unreclaimable`, which the receiver reports as parts of the others. That fallback is the default case, and it reads a little low: the receiver's feature gate that reads Linux's `MemAvailable` is in beta, so on by default, and with it `used` is the total less available memory, so the states add up to a little more than the total. (With the gate off, `used` is the total less free, buffers, and cache, and the states add up to the total exactly.) `system.memory.utilization` divides by the true total. The bytes chart is `system.memory.usage`'s `used`.
- **Filesystem used, per mount point:** read-only filesystems (`mode` `ro`) are left out: a snap package's squashfs image is always 100% used, so on Ubuntu the fullest would always be a snap. Then `system.filesystem.utilization` when sent (its `used` state, if it has states as the conventions define them); otherwise `system.filesystem.usage`'s `used` over all its states (used, free, and reserved): the share of the whole filesystem. Two charts of `system.filesystem.usage` are read, one of its `used` points and one of all, and divided line by line. Past eight mount points, the rest are added up as one line, as a split chart's are.
- **Network and disk:** `system.network.io` and `system.disk.io` per second, by direction, every device the receiver reads added up. The receiver reads the loopback interface, containers' virtual interfaces, whole disks with their partitions, and device-mapper and loop devices too, so without exclusions loopback traffic counts and a disk's bytes count two or three times (`sda`, `sda1`, and the `dm-0` on it). The configuration below leaves them out.
- **Load:** the 1, 5, and 15-minute load averages, each bucket's average, read in one query (`name IN (…)`, a line per metric).

The list shows each host's latest values in the period:

- a gauge's latest point per series (utilization, load), shares worked out as above;
- `system.cpu.time`: each series' increase between its last two points when both are of one run and the value did not go down (a delta point's value is its increase); the window is the 15 minutes before the host's last point of any host metric, and that point may come from another scraper up to one interval later than the CPU's, so a scrape interval up to 7.5 minutes (half the window) leaves the CPU's last two points in it. Past that, the list shows no CPU for a host that sends only `system.cpu.time` (one `system.cpu.utilization` point is enough), and its page's charts still do. The demo seed's hosts send every 15 minutes, then every 5 for the last quarter hour, so its run at any minute shows CPU (`seed-telemetry.test.ts`);
- per state, the latest value across the host's series of a metric (`argMax`), not their sum: after a Collector is upgraded, the old and new series (their scopes differ) both have recent points, and added up the memory share could pass 100%. A CPU's series stay apart, one per CPU, and are added up;
- the fullest filesystem: the share per mount point, read-only ones left out, then the highest, with its mount point.

A host is stale when nothing arrived from it in the 5 minutes before the request. That says something only while the period runs to now: for a period that ended before, the answer's `stale` is null and the page shows no badge.

### Routes

```text
GET /api/projects/:id/hosts?period=7d&environment=…
GET /api/projects/:id/hosts/charts?host=<host.name>&period=7d&environment=…
```

- **The list** makes three queries. The first finds the hosts and when each was last seen, from the host metrics' names (`name IN (…)`, so with the project ID a prefix of the sort key: only those metrics' rows are read), at most 500, by name; the answer says when there were more. The second lists the environments, as the other pages do. The third reads the latest values of the hosts listed, and only of the metrics the list shows (CPU, memory, filesystem, and the 1-minute load). Its time condition is each host's own window, from 15 minutes before its last point to that point, merged where they overlap, as ranges of the sort key (`(timestamp >= a AND timestamp < b) OR …`); each row is then kept only within its own host's window (`transform(host, hosts, cutoffs, 0)`). A host that stopped early in a long period thus reads its last minutes, not the period since. Measured on the development machine with 100 hosts sending 8 series a minute for 2 days, one of which stopped after its first hour (2.28 million rows): the query read 2,273,248 rows in 390 ms when bounded by the earliest window, and reads 86,860 rows in 30 ms by each host's. The first two queries still read the whole period's rows of those metrics, as finding the hosts needs.
- **A host's charts** make at most 10 chart queries and one for when it was last seen: each chart ranks its series (at most 1,000) inside its own query, rather than in a query of its own as the Metrics page does, since one host has few series. Each reads within its metric's rows of the project.
- A host name is refused past 4 KB, as ingestion cuts attribute values there.

All carry the dashboard's settings and the limit on queries at once ([how the pages read](#how-the-pages-read)).

### The Collector

The configuration the Hosts page shows, with the instance's address, while a project has sent no host metrics:

```yaml
receivers:
  host_metrics:
    collection_interval: 1m
    scrapers:
      cpu:
        metrics:
          system.cpu.utilization:
            enabled: true
      memory:
        metrics:
          system.memory.utilization:
            enabled: true
      filesystem:
        exclude_fs_types:
          fs_types: [squashfs]
          match_type: strict
        exclude_mount_points:
          mount_points: ["^/snap/.*$"]
          match_type: regexp
        metrics:
          system.filesystem.utilization:
            enabled: true
      network:
        exclude:
          interfaces: ["^(lo|veth.*|docker.*|br-.*)$"]
          match_type: regexp
      disk:
        exclude:
          devices:
            - "^(loop|ram|sr|dm-)[0-9]*$"
            - "^(sd|vd|xvd|hd)[a-z]+[0-9]+$"
            - "^(nvme[0-9]+n[0-9]+|mmcblk[0-9]+)p[0-9]+$"
          match_type: regexp
      load:

processors:
  resource_detection:
    detectors: [system]
    system:
      hostname_sources: [os]
  batch:
    send_batch_max_size: 8192

exporters:
  otlp_http:
    endpoint: https://analytics.example.com
    headers:
      Authorization: Bearer sk_…

service:
  pipelines:
    metrics:
      receivers: [host_metrics]
      processors: [resource_detection, batch]
      exporters: [otlp_http]
```

- **Names:** the Collector's components were renamed with underscores (`host_metrics`, `resource_detection`, `otlp_http`); older releases know them as `hostmetrics`, `resourcedetection`, and `otlphttp`. The exporter's and the processor's documentation keep the old names as deprecated aliases.
- **Utilization metrics:** on, so CPU, memory, and filesystem shares are the receiver's own, divided by the true totals.
- **Filesystems:** snap packages' read-only squashfs images and their `/snap/…` mount points are left out (the pages also leave out any read-only filesystem).
- **Network:** the loopback interface and containers' (`veth…`, `docker…`, `br-…`) are left out, so only traffic in and out of the machine counts.
- **Disks:** loop, RAM, optical, and device-mapper devices, and partitions (`sda1`, `nvme0n1p1`, `mmcblk0p1`) are left out, so each disk's bytes count once, from the whole disk. The patterns are anchored (`^…$`), so they mean the same whether the receiver matches a whole name or a part of one.
- **`host.name`:** the resource detection processor's `system` detector sets it. `hostname_sources: [os]` takes the operating system's name; its default tries DNS first, for a fully qualified name.
- **Batches:** `send_batch_max_size` bounds each request ([limits](#limits)).
- **In a container,** the receiver needs the host's root filesystem mounted and named with `root_path` (such as `/hostfs`), or it measures the container.
- **The key** is the project's secret key, under "Your server" in its settings; the page shows `sk_…` in its place and never prints the key.

## Not in the first version

- Charts that combine metrics, such as a cache's hit ratio from its hit and miss counters; saturation on the Hosts pages beyond the load average.
- gRPC, and OTLP from browsers.
- Exponential histograms and summaries (refused, and counted in `partial_success`).
- Profiles, sampling.
- Retention chosen per project.
- Linking a browser's page load to the backend trace it started (`traceparent`).
- Searching log bodies for a phrase: each word is matched on its own ([above](#how-the-pages-read)).
- Filtering traces by an attribute, and a trace's whole span count in the list (it counts the trace's spans in the period, an hour either side).
- "With errors" over more than the latest 10,000 failed spans of a period.
- Splitting a metric by a resource attribute or by service, filtering a chart by an attribute on the Metrics page (the Hosts pages filter by `host.name`), and a histogram's series with other bounds in the same chart.
- On the Hosts pages: swap, paging, disk operations and time, per-CPU and per-interface charts, processes (`process.*` metrics), and a total memory from `system.memory.limit`. Each can be charted alone on the Metrics page when the receiver sends it.
- A route's request methods apart: `GET` and `POST` of one `http.route` are one route.
- On the Databases pages: a database's server address (two servers with one database name are one database), statements with their literals kept for one call (a slow call's span holds its text), and alert rules on a query shape.
